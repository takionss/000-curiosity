When processing these stacks in software like AutoStakkert or Registax, beginners often fall into the trap of over-sharpening, which creates harsh artificial artifacts around the storm's edges. *Gently tease out the details with multi-scale wavelets rather than blasting the image with aggressive contrast sliders.*

As you refine your workflow over multiple opposition seasons, keep a detailed observation log noting the seeing conditions, filter combinations, and exact UTC timestamps for every session. ### <span style="color: #E74C3C;">Q1. How can I keep my telescope optics from dewing up during long nights of tracking Jupiter's Great Red Spot?</span>



**A:** Dealing with overnight moisture is one of the most frustrating hurdles when you are standing outside for hours trying to catch optimal seeing conditions. When I first started serious planetary imaging, I lost half my best data sessions because my corrector plate or eyepiece would fog over right when the planet reached its highest point in the sky.

To prevent this, you should always use a proper dew shield and invest in a reliable, adjustable dew heater strip wrapped snugly around your optical tube. Never leave your gear completely exposed without a power source for your heating bands, as once moisture settles on glass, cooling down and clearing it ruins your thermal equilibrium. *Always power up your heating strips before condensation forms rather than trying to dry wet optics after the fact.*





### <span style="color: #FF5733;">Q2. What is the best way to determine the exact transit time of the Great Red Spot for my specific geographical location?</span>



**A:** Trying to line up your observing schedule with the storm's transit across Jupiter's central meridian can feel like playing a guessing game if you rely on guesswork. In my early days, I walked outside blindly, only to find that the storm had already rotated out of view an hour prior.

You need to use specialized planetarium software or reliable online ephemeris calculators that account for your exact longitude and time zone adjustments. Keep in mind that Jupiter's rapid rotation rate means the planet spins completely on its axis in less than ten hours, giving you a very narrow window to catch the storm dead-center. *Always check updated transit tables on the night of your observation to match the planet's actual drift rate.*
